## Part 1 — The conversation, verbatim

1. F&G didn’t initially buy 100% of SYNCIS. In January 2023, F&G announced that it acquired a significant minority equity stake in SYNCIS; third-party transaction data reports the stake as 49%. The reason matters, because it gives you a pretty good look at how a carrier thinks about distribution. F&G’s CEO said the investment was intended to diversify F&G’s earnings, deepen its existing partnership with SYNCIS, and capture what F&G saw as untapped demand for permanent life insurance among multicultural and middle-market consumers. F&G also described the transaction as accretive to shareholders. But strategically, I’d translate that into something simpler: F&G was buying access to distribution. SYNCIS had built something that is extremely valuable to a life carrier: a large independent distribution organization capable of recruiting agents, training them and consistently putting insurance business in front of consumers. SYNCIS specifically focuses on financial protection for families and has built its associate force around reaching ordinary households rather than just affluent wealth-management clients. For F&G, owning part of that machine potentially gives them several advantages: 1. More predictable premium flow. Instead of merely hoping an IMO sends them business, F&G participates economically in the distributor producing it. 2. Stronger carrier loyalty. If F&G owns 49% of an IMO, the relationship is structurally deeper than a normal carrier/IMO contract. 3. Access to a demographic F&G wanted. F&G explicitly called out multicultural and middle-market consumers and permanent life insurance as an underserved opportunity. 4. A second earnings stream. F&G can potentially earn money both as the manufacturer of insurance products and through its ownership interest in the distribution company. 5. Strategic intelligence. A close relationship with a major IMO gives the carrier much better visibility into what agents sell, what consumers respond to, recruiting trends and which products are gaining traction. And this is the part I think is especially relevant to Thrive: A carrier doesn’t necessarily need to acquire an agency because the agency has amazing technology or intellectual property. Distribution itself can become the asset. If Thrive eventually controls thousands of productive agents and hundreds of millions—or billions—of dollars of annual premium flow, you aren’t merely an insurance agency anymore. You become a distribution platform that carriers cannot easily replicate internally. That dramatically changes the negotiating dynamic. Instead of: “Can we get a better contract?” it becomes: “We control $2B of annual premium placement. What are you willing to do to become one of our strategic carrier partners?” That can mean enhanced contracts, product exclusivity, custom product design, marketing support, lead subsidies, financing, equity investments, joint ventures—or eventually an acquisition. There’s another important nuance: F&G didn’t need 100% ownership. A 49% stake lets the carrier participate economically and secure the strategic relationship while the entrepreneurs operating SYNCIS can still retain substantial ownership and incentive. That model is potentially much more interesting for you than simply “building Thrive to sell it.” You could conceivably build Thrive to a point where a carrier says: “We’ll put $100M+ into the company for 20–40% because we want permanent access to this distribution.” You retain control, take significant liquidity, capitalize further expansion, and potentially make the remaining equity substantially more valuable. If you want, I can also break down what F&G likely saw in SYNCIS financially and what Thrive would have to look like for a carrier to make the same type of investment in us.

2. Yes — SYNCIS was much more than a basic term-life shop. Your instinct is right. The key nuance is that F&G itself described SYNCIS as a major driver of its IUL business. When F&G bought 49% in January 2023, it said SYNCIS had been a distribution partner for nearly 15 years and had materially contributed to F&G becoming a top-three indexed universal life provider by number of policies issued in 2022. So their value proposition was closer to advanced-market life distribution, especially permanent life/IUL, than a simple high-volume final-expense or term operation. They also operated in the broader “risk and retirement” market, which is where annuities naturally fit, though the acquisition announcement specifically emphasizes IUL and permanent life much more heavily than annuity production. That actually makes the F&G deal more interesting for Thrive. F&G wasn’t just buying an agent headcount. They were buying into a distributor capable of producing higher-value, longer-duration insurance liabilities for the carrier. IUL is particularly attractive strategically because one productive relationship can create large recurring premium flows and substantial carrier economics over decades. So I would characterize SYNCIS roughly like this: Recruiting/distribution machine → middle-market families → permanent life/IUL → retirement/advanced-market opportunities. And that’s potentially a very relevant blueprint for Thrive. If you combine massive agent distribution with an advanced-markets layer — IUL, annuities, premium finance where appropriate, business-owner planning, key person, buy-sell, executive benefits, estate cases — your production per agent and strategic value to carriers can rise dramatically versus staying predominantly mortgage protection/term/final expense. In fact, F&G later explicitly characterized the SYNCIS investment as part of its strategy of building “owned distribution” and called it a capital-light, accretive way to grow. That is probably the bigger lesson: the ultimate agency isn’t just a huge field force. It’s a huge field force with an advanced-market conversion engine sitting above it. For example, Thrive could eventually have 5,000 agents generating the household relationships, while a centralized 100–200-person advanced-markets desk converts the best opportunities into $100K, $250K, $500K+ premium IUL/annuity/business cases. That architecture could be enormously more valuable to a carrier than simply having 5,000 agents writing ordinary protection business.

3. [Owner] So with what we do at thrive companies, we do inbound calls for General life insurance policies under 50k in death benefit usually. How can we get an exit? Can we even get an exit in the final expense market like this? We have a lead company called lead tech. We have documented systems. We have office spaces and distribution. But syncis was a key distributor for F&G. The question is, how should we build Thrive companies to sell for the highest multiple while moving the fastest.

4. Yes. Final expense is absolutely sellable. There is direct precedent: AmeriLife has acquired final-expense distributors such as Maximum Senior Benefits and Equita, specifically citing their final-expense distribution, training and call-center capabilities; Integrity has also acquired multiple agencies with substantial final-expense production. The bigger question is what kind of company Thrive becomes before you sell it. Right now, based on what you described, I would think about Thrive as four potentially separate assets: Thrive Companies → consumer acquisition / Lead Tech → centralized inbound sales infrastructure → agent distribution + offices → operating system / training / management systems That combination can become much more valuable than a normal final-expense IMO. The mistake I would avoid Don’t optimize Thrive around: “How do we become the biggest final-expense agency?” Optimize around: “How do we become the most valuable insurance distribution platform serving the mass-market consumer?” That’s a very different company. Final expense should be your wedge, not necessarily your identity. There are already strategic buyers who have demonstrated that final-expense distribution has acquisition value. AmeriLife, for example, explicitly acquired Equita partly to expand its final-expense platform and call-center capabilities. And AmeriLife has continued moving deeper into life distribution—closing its acquisition of Crump Life Insurance Services in 2025. So there is a real buyer universe. But I think you can build something significantly more valuable than a conventional FE agency. What I would build Think about Thrive eventually looking like this: THRIVE COMPANIES 1. Lead Tech — Consumer Acquisition Engine: Own the customer before anyone else does. Lead Tech should eventually control: paid social, search, native, direct mail, inbound phone, SEO, remarketing, data enrichment, call routing, lead scoring, creative production, attribution. Most IMOs don’t really control demand. They recruit agents who then go buy leads somewhere. That’s fragile. If Thrive owns the consumer acquisition machine, a buyer isn’t just getting producers. They’re buying: Customer → lead → inbound call → agent → policy → carrier. That is considerably harder to replicate. I would eventually want Lead Tech generating substantially more demand than Thrive itself can consume. Then you have external proof: “Our demand engine generated $X billion of premium opportunities, of which Thrive distribution consumed Y%.” Now Lead Tech has standalone value too. 2. Thrive Distribution — National Sales Machine: The second moat is distribution. Not: 5,000 contracted agents. I don’t care nearly as much about that number. I’d rather have: 2,000 active writers producing every month. Buyers will look underneath recruiting numbers. They’ll want to know: active agent count, premium per active agent, agent tenure, first-sale time, 30/60/90-day retention, manager productivity, agent acquisition cost, time-to-productivity, placement, persistency, chargebacks, carrier concentration, geography, compliance. This is where your office model could become valuable. Imagine telling an acquirer: Thrive has 42 offices. Every office follows the same operating system. We can launch a market with one leader and 12 agents and grow it to 50 productive agents within 12 months. That’s not real estate. That’s a replicable geographic expansion model. Very different. 3. Thrive OS: This is where all the systems you’ve been documenting matter. But don’t think: “We have SOPs, therefore we’re worth more.” Every seller says that. You need to demonstrate: The company produces similar results regardless of who operates the system. For example: New agent enters Thrive. Day 0 → licensing/contracts. Day 3 → training. Day 5 → call certification.

5. Day 7 → live inbound calls. Day 10 → first application. Day 30 → $X AP. Day 90 → $Y AP. And those numbers should be measurable across thousands of cohorts. Then your operating system becomes real intellectual property. A PE buyer loves something that can be copied. 4. Build the management layer before you need it: This might be the single most important thing for your eventual multiple. If Andrew disappears for 90 days, what happens? If production collapses: You own a job. If production barely moves: You own a company. Eventually Thrive needs: CEO, COO, CFO, Chief Distribution Officer, Chief Marketing Officer / Lead Tech President, VP Carrier Relations, VP Compliance, VP Recruiting, VP Training, Regional Presidents, Office Leaders. And those people should actually operate the business. Your job eventually becomes capital allocation, strategy, senior recruiting and carrier relationships. That makes Thrive transferable. 5. Make the economics beautiful: Growth alone won’t create the best exit. You want growth plus predictable EBITDA. Insurance brokerage M&A remains very active. Broad insurance brokerage transactions involving businesses with more than $1M of EBITDA averaged about 11.8× EBITDA during the first half of 2025, according to Sica Fletcher data. That benchmark isn’t directly transferable to final-expense agencies—the revenue quality and business models differ—but it illustrates how strongly buyers reward high-quality insurance distribution earnings. The massive AssuredPartners transaction is another extreme example: Gallagher agreed to acquire it for $13.45B, or roughly 14.3× adjusted pretax earnings. Again, that’s a diversified commercial brokerage rather than a final-expense comparator, but it demonstrates what scaled, institutional insurance distribution can command. So I wouldn’t obsess over getting Thrive to some arbitrary revenue number. I’d obsess over: EBITDA quality. Imagine: $500M AP / $100M revenue / $30M normalized EBITDA versus: $500M AP / $65M revenue / $4M EBITDA. Those are completely different assets.

6. Become carrier-important: This is where your SYNCIS observation becomes relevant. You don’t necessarily need to become F&G’s largest IUL distributor. You need to become strategically important to somebody. Imagine Thrive reaches: Carrier A $300M AP/year, Carrier B $200M, Carrier C $150M, Carrier D $100M. Then you develop proprietary products with them. Eventually perhaps: “Thrive Final Expense Series powered by Carrier X.” Now you’ve crossed another line. You’re not merely distributing whatever products carriers give everyone. You’re influencing product architecture. That makes the carrier relationship harder to replace.

7. Move beyond final expense — but carefully: I would not abandon final expense. Quite the opposite. Become terrifyingly good at it first. Then use the customer relationship to expand vertically. Your customer might initially call about: $15,000 burial policy. But that household may also need: Medicare, annuity, mortgage protection, term, IUL, long-term care, retirement income, supplemental health. So your strategic evolution could be: Final Expense → Senior Life → Medicare → Retirement → Annuities → Advanced Life. That drastically increases customer lifetime value. AmeriLife’s acquisition strategy illustrates this broader model: it describes itself as distributing life, health, annuity and retirement-planning solutions, while still acquiring dedicated final-expense platforms. That’s the direction I’d study. The hidden gold mine: your customers. This may become bigger than your agents. Suppose Thrive eventually writes: 500,000 policies annually. After several years you could have millions of households in your ecosystem. Now imagine knowing, compliantly: age, state, policy type, premium, home ownership, family structure, retirement status, contact history, product eligibility. You can build a massive insurance household graph. Then Thrive isn’t continually paying Meta $80 to reacquire essentially the same demographic. You start monetizing the existing household. That’s where EBITDA can explode. The company I would want to sell: I wouldn’t bring buyers: “We have 8,000 final-expense agents.” I’d bring them this: THRIVE COMPANIES — $1.2B annual premium, 8,000 contracted agents, 3,400 monthly active writers, 70 offices, 48 states. Lead Tech — 4M+ proprietary inbound consumers/year, $120M annual advertising spend managed, proprietary call-routing/attribution/data infrastructure. Thrive OS — standardized recruiting, training, quality assurance, compliance, management, office launch system. Consumer Platform — 1.8M households, FE life, Medicare, annuities, advanced markets. Financials — $300M revenue, $90M EBITDA, 30% EBITDA margin, 40% 3-year CAGR. Concentration — largest carrier <25%, largest lead channel <30%, founder dependence low. Now the conversation isn’t: “What’s a final-expense agency worth?” It’s: “Who can afford to let someone else acquire Thrive?” That’s where competitive tension comes from. Who could buy something like that? Strategic distributors (AmeriLife-type, Integrity-type), insurance carriers (particularly if Thrive becomes disproportionately important to their new-business production), private equity (especially once you have $20M–$50M+ of clean EBITDA and a credible path to doubling it), alternative asset managers / insurance platforms. And the best situation is having all four groups interested simultaneously. Then you run a competitive process. I would probably NOT sell Thrive once. Your optimal wealth-creation path may look more like: Stage 1 — founder owned: $0 → $10M EBITDA, 100% ownership. Stage 2 — minority recap: $10M → $30M EBITDA, sell perhaps 20–30%, take meaningful cash off the table, bring in institutional capital, keep control. Stage 3 — institutional scale: $30M → $100M EBITDA, acquire smaller distributors, expand offices, build Medicare/annuity/advanced markets, potentially recap again. Stage 4 — strategic exit: sell controlling interest at enormous scale, keep rollover equity if the partner is right. The founders of many large insurance distribution platforms create wealth through multiple liquidity events, not one binary sale. If speed + highest multiple are both the objective, I would spend the next 30 months almost entirely building six numbers: 1. Monthly AP. 2. Normalized EBITDA. 3. Active agents. 4. Agent productivity. 5. Owned-lead percentage. 6. Customer LTV / CAC. And three moats: Demand. Distribution. Systems. Everything else is secondary. Your offices matter if they increase recruiting/productivity/retention. Your SOPs matter if they create repeatable performance. Lead Tech matters if it lowers CAC and gives you proprietary consumer flow. Your agents matter if they consistently produce and remain with Thrive. That’s the transformation I would make: Final expense is the product Thrive sells today. Insurance distribution should be the business Thrive actually builds. Consumer acquisition should be the moat. The operating system should make it scalable. And cash flow should make it valuable. If you execute that correctly, I don’t think the endgame needs to be a $50M–$100M agency sale. You can architect Thrive toward becoming an institutional insurance distribution platform worth hundreds of millions or potentially $1B+, but that requires building the financial profile and diversification to justify that valuation rather than assuming a brokerage-market multiple automatically applies to final expense. And given the trajectory you’re talking about—moving from roughly $1M monthly AP toward $25M monthly AP—I would actually build the “Thrive $1 Billion Exit Architecture” backward from the buyer’s diligence checklist, then make every major decision for the next 2.5 years against that scorecard.

## Part 2 — Analysis (this repository's contribution, 2026-08-15)

### What was verified against primary sources

- **F&G / SYNCIS, confirmed.** F&G announced a **49% equity stake** in SYNCIS
  on **January 31, 2023** — an ~1,200-agent IMO focused on multicultural
  markets (Korean, African-American, Persian per coverage), a ~15-year
  distribution partner, credited by F&G as contributing to its **top-3 IUL
  rank by policies issued in 2022**, framed by F&G as "capital-light,
  accretive **owned distribution**." Every load-bearing claim the document
  makes about this deal is accurate.
- **Gallagher / AssuredPartners, confirmed with a nuance the document
  omits.** $13.45B gross at **14.3× pro-forma EBITDAC** — but net of a $1B
  deferred tax asset and post-synergy, Gallagher itself framed it as
  **~11.3×**. The headline multiple is the marketing number.
- **Not verified tonight** (plausible, consistent with the market, but
  unchecked): AmeriLife's Maximum Senior Benefits and Equita acquisitions,
  the Crump close date, and the Sica Fletcher 11.8× H1-2025 figure. Treat as
  claims until sourced.

### What the document gets right (keep, and build against)

Wedge-not-identity. The four-asset decomposition (demand / distribution /
operating system / customer base). Active writers over contracted headcount.
EBITDA quality over revenue. Multiple liquidity events over one binary sale.
The 49%-model insight — a carrier check that buys liquidity *without* buying
control. And the closing instruction, which is the best sentence in it: build
backward from the buyer's diligence checklist.

### The five real gaps (labeled: these are this repository's judgments)

1. **The multiples anchor the reader to the wrong market.** 11.8× and 14.3×
   are commercial P&C **brokerage** — recurring-commission revenue that
   renews itself. Final-expense/life IMO earnings are first-year-commission
   dominated, chargeback-exposed, and renewal-thin; that revenue quality has
   historically transacted at materially lower multiples, with the premium
   band reachable only at platform scale, diversification, and clean
   persistency. The document says "not directly transferable" once, then
   quotes the big numbers twice. The honest framing: those multiples are the
   **prize for ceasing to be a final-expense agency**, not a benchmark for
   being one. Every point of the plan should be read as "what earns the next
   turn of multiple."

2. **Persistency and chargebacks are the valuation, not a bullet point.**
   For sub-$50k inbound telesales FE, the first three diligence questions
   are 13-month persistency by cohort, placement rate, and chargeback
   history — before anyone opens the growth deck. A fast-growing book
   flatters itself: young vintages haven't had time to lapse, so blended
   persistency overstates reality precisely when growth is fastest. Start
   capturing **cohort vintage curves now**, monthly, from carrier reports.
   Two identical-revenue Thrives with 58% vs 78% thirteen-month persistency
   are different companies by a factor, not a discount.

3. **TCPA and consent are absent from the document entirely — and Lead Tech
   concentrates them.** Owning the demand engine means owning the
   telemarketing liability: consent capture and storage, DNC hygiene,
   call-recording consent by state, carrier-complaint ratios, licensing
   coverage. At the described scale ($120M ad spend, 4M inbound consumers) a
   buyer's legal diligence on this can reprice or kill a deal outright. The
   moat needs a compliance spine or the moat *is* the liability. (This
   repository already builds in that direction: the portal's consent-gated
   recording access and append-only audit are early pieces of exactly the
   evidence a buyer will ask for.)

4. **The SYNCIS analogy has a limit the document soft-pedals.** F&G bought
   IUL distribution — long-duration, high-margin liabilities carriers crave
   for decades. Sub-$50k simplified-issue FE is a different liability class:
   small face, lapse-heavy, less strategically scarce to a carrier. The
   carrier-equity endgame (the $100M-for-30% check) therefore **depends on**
   the vertical expansion into Medicare/annuities/advanced life. In the
   document it reads as an enhancement; in reality it is a prerequisite for
   that particular exit door.

5. **The growth bridge is unmodeled.** $1M → $25M monthly AP is 25× in ~30
   months. Nothing in the document bridges it: writers needed at a given
   AP-per-active-writer, lead volume at real contact and close rates, ad
   spend at real CPL, licensing throughput, manager pipeline, and the cash
   cycle (advance commissions fund growth early; chargeback reserves claw it
   back on a lag). The aspirational scorecard also assumes a **42.5% active
   ratio** (3,400 of 8,000) — top-decile-or-better versus industry norms.
   Fine as a target; label it as one. An unmodeled 25× is a wish with a
   deadline.

   Also, on "the hidden gold mine": *"knowing, compliantly"* is one word
   carrying an entire legal department. Cross-selling Medicare requires
   separate certification; monetizing the household graph requires consent
   captured at the point of sale and real data governance. Budget for that
   before counting that EBITDA.

### Bottom line

The document's architecture is sound and its facts held up under
verification. Its two blind spots are the ones that kill FE exits in
practice — persistency vintage quality and telemarketing compliance — and
its multiple expectations need re-anchoring from brokerage comps to "what
each transformation stage actually earns." Adopt the scorecard, instrument
it through the portal, and make Mr. T's "honesty of numbers" discipline the
diligence-readiness standard: a data room is a punch list you already pass.
